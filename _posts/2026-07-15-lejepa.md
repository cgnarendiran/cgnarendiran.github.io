*On the cover: a poured melt, cored along two dozen random directions at once. Decorative, and also roughly what SIGReg does a thousand times a step.*

[Part 1](/blog/jepa-nobody-cares-about-the-wallpaper/) left us in an interrogation room. We had stopped asking models to repaint the scene and started asking them to describe it, we threw the wallpaper away, and the frozen-probe numbers said the trade was a good one.

Then we looked at what was holding the whole thing upright. An exponential moving average teacher. A stop-gradient. A predictor kept deliberately too weak to do the job alone. A centering and sharpening schedule that somebody, at some point, tuned.

Not one of those forbids the model from answering "a person, probably" to every question ever asked of it. They only make it unlikely, for reasons that took a small literature to half-explain.

Which makes the state of the art a workshop. The master smith turns out beautiful blades. He quenches at a temperature his grandfather chose, hammers the billet exactly three hundred times, and cannot tell you which of those steps is load-bearing. Skip one and the blade cracks in the fire. Skip another and nothing happens at all. Everybody keeps hammering.

Welcome to the era of **LeJEPA**. This is the story of how somebody finally wrote down the spec sheet.

## The charms are not free

It is tempting to file all this under aesthetics. The blades come out fine, the papers get published, who cares if the recipe is folklore.

Three things care.

The first is that the charms are coupled. Teacher decay interacts with the sharpening temperature interacts with the warmup length interacts with how narrow you made the predictor, and moving one means re-tuning the others. That is not a hyperparameter, it is a hyperparameter *surface*, and you cross-validate over it with runs that cost GPU-months.

They also do not transfer. A recipe tuned for ViT-L on ImageNet is not a recipe for a ResNet on satellite imagery, and the failure mode is not "slightly worse", it is a loss curve that flatlines two days in.

The third is the one that actually hurts. **You cannot read the training loss.** In a teacher-student setup the loss falls whether the model is learning something or quietly collapsing toward the constant answer, so the only way to know if a run worked is to finish it, freeze the encoder, attach a probe and evaluate on labels. Self-supervised learning, whose entire pitch is that you do not need labels, could not tell you if it was working without labels.

So the barrier to entry was never really the compute. It was that you had to be able to afford being wrong a hundred times.

## The anatomy of a LeJEPA

Strip a joint-embedding method down and every one of them is doing two jobs at once. Pull the views of the same image together. Stop everything from piling into the same corner.

Family one from part 1 does the second job with machinery: the EMA copy, the stop-gradient, the crippled predictor. [Barlow Twins](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.03230) and [VICReg](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.04906) do it with statistics instead, and I said at the time to hold that thought.

Here it is. **LeJEPA** ([Balestriero and LeCun, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.08544)) keeps the second family's shape and replaces the hand-picked statistics with the one distribution you can prove is the right target:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{LeJEPA}} = (1-\lambda)\, \mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}} + \lambda\, \mathcal{L}_{\text{SIGReg}}
$$

where $\lambda \in (0,1)$ is the only knob in the objective. The prediction term is as plain as it gets. Take $V$ augmented views of an image, push each through the same encoder and projector to get $z_1 \dots z_V$, and pull each one toward the average of the others:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}} = \frac{1}{V}\sum_{v=1}^{V} \big\| z_v - \bar{z} \big\|^2, \qquad \bar{z} = \frac{1}{V}\sum_{u=1}^{V} z_u
$$

Look at $\bar{z}$ for a second. It is not detached. Gradients flow back through every view, including the one being used as the target, which is precisely the thing every method since [BYOL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.07733) said you must never do.

![Two encoder branches sharing weights feed into a prediction term and a SIGReg term, with a line of crossed-out components below](/images/blog29/lejepa-architecture.png) *Figure 1: LeJEPA. One encoder, used on every view, with gradients flowing through all of them. Everything listed along the bottom used to be mandatory. The second loss term is what replaced the lot. Source: Author*

**NOTE:** the second term operates on the projector output, not the encoder output. The features you actually keep and use downstream sit one layer upstream of the thing being made Gaussian. Everybody in this family does that, and nobody has a clean story for why it helps.

## What a good description should look like

You are casting a part and nobody has told you what it will be bolted to. Could be a hinge. Could be a bracket taking a load straight down. You know how much metal you have and nothing else.

What shape do you pour?

Not a long thin one. The moment the load arrives from an angle you did not anticipate, the part is a wire. You pour something with the same strength in every direction, because you are not betting on a direction, and this is the entire theoretical content of the paper dressed in overalls.

Formally, fix the total variance budget of the embedding distribution, the trace of its covariance, since otherwise you can cheat by making everything bigger. Then over the space of downstream tasks you might face, the isotropic Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)$ uniquely minimises the integrated bias of linear, kernel and $k$-NN probes. Isotropy handles the direction gamble. Gaussianity handles the variance term, and it is also the [maximum-entropy](/blog/cross-entropy-loss/) distribution for a given covariance, which is a polite way of saying it is the shape that commits to nothing beyond what you asked for.

Brace yourselves, here is the arithmetic that makes it concrete.

Take a 512-dimensional embedding and a variance budget of 512 units. Cast A puts 64 units into each of 8 directions and zero into the remaining 504. Cast B puts 1 unit into every direction. Now a downstream task shows up needing to read off some direction $w$ that you did not know about at training time. Under Cast A, the chance that $w$ lands anywhere near the 8 directions carrying signal is about 1.6%, and in the other 98.4% of cases your probe is reading a constant and learning nothing. Under Cast B, every direction it could possibly ask for has variance 1.

![Two point clouds with the same total variance, one squashed flat and one round, each showing its spread along a vertical probe direction](/images/blog29/isotropy-argument.png) *Figure 2: The same amount of metal, poured two ways. Both clouds have the same total variance. The flat one has 0.15 of spread along the direction the downstream task happened to want; the round one has 0.99, and would have 0.99 along any other direction you picked. Source: Author*

Real anisotropy is graded rather than binary, so the real numbers are less lurid than 1.6%. The ordering does not change.

## SIGReg, or: you cannot X-ray an ingot

Fine. Aim for the isotropic Gaussian. Now check whether you got there.

This is where it should fall apart. Testing whether a cloud of points in 512 dimensions follows a particular multivariate distribution is a famously hopeless problem, the batch you are testing has maybe 512 points in it, and you need to do this on every step of training for a hundred epochs.

The way out is a theorem from 1936. **Cramér–Wold** says a distribution on $\mathbb{R}^D$ is completely determined by the distributions of its one-dimensional projections $w^\top z$ over all unit vectors $w$. Which specialises to exactly what we need: $z$ is isotropic Gaussian if and only if *every* one of its 1D shadows is the same univariate $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$, whichever way you point.

You cannot X-ray the ingot. You can take a core sample, and if the core is right in every direction, so is the ingot.

### Step 1: the core sample

Draw $K$ random directions, sample them Gaussian and normalise each to unit length, stack them into $A \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times K}$. Project the batch: $P = ZA$. One matmul, and $K$ is 1024 in the paper's ImageNet configs, 256 in their minimal example.

You do not check every direction, obviously, there are infinitely many. You draw a fresh $K$ of them on every step, seeded off the global step so that all GPUs pick the same ones. Over a run you cover the sphere in expectation, and the model never gets to learn which directions the inspector prefers.

### Step 2: tap it and listen

Now the univariate test. The characteristic function of a distribution is its Fourier transform, $\varphi(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{itX}]$, and you can think of it as the sound a sample makes when you tap it: two distributions are identical exactly when they ring the same at every frequency. For the standard normal the ring is $\varphi(t) = e^{-t^2/2}$, which is clean and known in advance and needs no estimating.

The **Epps–Pulley** statistic measures the gap between the sample's ring and that one:

$$
T = N \int \big| \hat{\varphi}(t) - e^{-t^2/2} \big|^2 \, e^{-t^2/2} \, dt
$$

where $N$ is the number of samples, $\hat{\varphi}(t) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} e^{i t p_n}$ is the empirical characteristic function of the projected values $p_n$, and the second $e^{-t^2/2}$ is a weight that says low frequencies count for more than high ones. In the implementation the integral runs over $t \in [0, 3]$ with 17 trapezoid knots, using the symmetry of the real and imaginary parts to do half the work.

![The target curve e to the minus t squared over two against a sample's empirical characteristic function, and the resulting integrand](/images/blog29/epps-pulley.png) *Figure 3: Left, the standard normal's frequency signature in black and a projected sample's in red, read at 17 points. Right, the weighted squared gap between them. Its area, times the batch size, is what gets added to the loss. Source: Author*

The library ships Anderson–Darling, Cramér–von Mises, Shapiro–Wilk, Jarque–Bera and Watson too, so the choice of Epps–Pulley is not arbitrary. It is a statistic built from $\cos$ and $\sin$ of the data, and those are bounded, which makes both the loss and its gradient bounded. Kolmogorov–Smirnov and friends have no such promise, and an unbounded penalty computed on a live minibatch is a spike waiting to happen. A test you run once on a finished dataset and a test you differentiate through four hundred thousand times are not the same job.

![A point cloud with seven random directions marked, and two of the projected histograms compared against a normal curve](/images/blog29/sigreg-slicing.png) *Figure 4: Slicing in action. Direction 1 gives a projection that passes for Gaussian and contributes almost nothing to the loss. Direction 2 catches the same cloud being two lumps and is where the gradient comes from. Source: Author*

### Step 3: the dial

Average the statistic over the $K$ directions, multiply by $\lambda$, add the prediction term times $(1 - \lambda)$, and that is the objective. $\lambda$ is 0.05 in the ImageNet runs and 0.02 in the small one. Fifty lines of code, and one number to choose.

There is a detail here I keep coming back to. To compute the statistic across a global batch spread over hundreds of GPUs, the only quantities that have to cross the wire are the per-knot means of $\cos(t p_n)$ and $\sin(t p_n)$. That is 34 floats per direction, no matter how large the batch is. The test is exact over every sample on every device, and it costs a few kilobytes of all-reduce.

## What that buys

The paper reports the recipe running out of the box across 60+ architectures, ResNets and ViTs and ConvNets, 10+ datasets, and up to 1.8B parameters, with no per-architecture tuning and no gradient clipping.

Here is the head-to-head against part 1's I-JEPA, averaged over eight transfer datasets at three labelling budgets:

| Model | Params | Epochs | 1-shot | 10-shot | all labels |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| LeJEPA ViT-L | 304M | 100 | 29.55 | **60.95** | **79.48** |
| LeJEPA ConvNeXtV2-H | 660M | 100 | **31.58** | 60.70 | 77.56 |
| I-JEPA ViT-H | 632M | 300 | 30.20 | 60.51 | 78.50 |

Nobody wins that table. The margins are under a point and I would not defend a single one of them.

The tie is the result. Half the parameters, a third of the epochs, and the entire apparatus of part 1 removed: no teacher, no stop-gradient, no predictor, no schedules. On ImageNet-1K a ViT-H/14 reaches 79% under a frozen linear probe, which is competitive rather than leading, and we will come back to that.

The number I would actually build on is a different one. The training loss correlates with downstream probe accuracy at **94%+ Spearman**. You can select a model by looking at the loss. No labels, no held-out probe, no finishing the run to find out. Every method in part 1 hands you a loss you have to ignore. This one hands you a gauge.

Which drops the price of admission. Their own worked example gets a ViT-S/8 to 90.7% on Imagenette in about 200 minutes on one GPU, no cluster involved. And in-domain pretraining stops being a doomed move: on a small specialised corpus like Galaxy10, roughly 11k images of galaxies, training from scratch beats transferring from DINOv2. Eleven thousand images beating a frontier foundation model is not a result the last five years prepared anyone for.

| Piece | I-JEPA and friends | LeJEPA | What it was for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Target encoder | EMA copy, $\tau \approx 0.996$–$0.999$ | none | stopping the branches agreeing on nothing |
| Gradient path | stop-grad on the target | gradients through all views | same |
| Predictor | narrow ViT, weak on purpose | none | same |
| Output schedule | centering and sharpening | none | same |
| What forbids collapse | nothing, empirically | the SIGReg term | — |
| Knobs in the loss | several, coupled | one, $\lambda$ | — |

And the ablation is the one you can do in your head. Set $\lambda = 0$ and you are left with the prediction term alone, whose global minimum is the constant embedding: representation collapse, exactly as derived in part 1, arriving on schedule. These models are lazy animals and the constant answer is the nearest couch. Every other method has to find that out empirically, one dead run at a time. This one tells you in advance.

## Where things are going

**LeWorldModel (2026)**: [LeWorldModel](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19312) drops SIGReg into an action-conditioned world model trained end to end from raw pixels, with a next-embedding prediction loss and the Gaussian regulariser as the only two terms. It reports cutting the tunable loss hyperparameters from six to one against the previous end-to-end alternative, runs at around 15M parameters on a single GPU, and plans up to 48x faster than world models built on foundation backbones. That is part 1's tier 3, the predictor conditioned on actions instead of position, reached without the machinery, and it is the cheap end of the [world-models-as-data-substrate](/blog/vla-pixels-to-tokens/) bet the VLA post ended on.

**Arguing with the target**: the isotropic Gaussian is now something people push back on, which is the healthiest possible sign. [Beyond Isotropy in JEPAs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.20107) proposes a Hamiltonian geometry instead, SPHERE-JEPA goes to the sphere, KerJEPA swaps the characteristic-function test for kernel discrepancies. A field with a wrong answer moves. A field with no answer just accumulates tricks.

**Conditions rather than claims**: [When Does LeJEPA Learn a World Model?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26379) asks the question part 1 spent a whole section being pedantic about, and asks it as a conditional rather than a slogan.

**Video**: LeVJEPA carries the recipe to clips, where the compute savings compound, since video is where the heuristic-tuning bill was always worst.

## The honest cons

"Without the heuristics" is oversold, and the gap is not small. LeJEPA still trains on 2 global crops and 6 local crops per image with colour jitter, grayscale, blur and solarisation, a pipeline lifted wholesale from DINO and tuned the old way. The theorem tells you what shape the cloud should be. It says nothing whatsoever about what should count as two views of the same thing, and that decision is doing at least as much work as anything the paper proved. The superstition moved house, it did not leave town.

79% against DINOv2's 86.5% on ImageNet linear probing is a real gap and the paper does not hide it. The argument on offer is provability and cost, not the leaderboard. Worth knowing which one you are buying.

The sliced test is weaker in high dimensions than the Cramér–Wold theorem makes it sound. Random 1D projections of almost any high-dimensional distribution drift toward Gaussian as $D$ grows, a result Diaconis and Freedman made precise in 1984, which means a cloud can sail through a great many 1D normality tests while being badly non-Gaussian in ways no single direction reveals.

![Histograms of a random one-dimensional projection of a uniform hypercube at three dimensions, becoming more bell-shaped as the dimension rises](/images/blog29/projection-power.png) *Figure 5: Points drawn uniformly from a hypercube, which is about as un-Gaussian as a distribution gets, projected onto one random direction. At $D=2$ the test would catch it instantly. At $D=128$ the projection is visually indistinguishable from the bell curve it is being compared against. Source: Author*

The theorem still holds, since it quantifies over *all* directions and you are sampling 1024 of them fresh each step. The paper's answer is that embeddings from a smooth network are not adversarial, so a modest number of slices has real power. I believe that and I would still like to see somebody map the failure mode instead of arguing about it.

And "provable" describes the target, not the trip. The theorem says an isotropic Gaussian embedding minimises downstream risk. It does not say gradient descent on this penalty arrives at one, and nothing here is a convergence guarantee. What changed is that the objective now names where it is going, which is a genuinely different situation from an objective whose minimum is the thing you are trying to avoid.

## Conclusion

Part 1 argued that reconstruction was the wrong ask: describe the room, do not repaint it, nobody cares about the wallpaper. It ended without being able to say what a good description was, only what a bad one looked like once you were staring at it.

This is the other half. A good description spreads itself evenly, because the task you will be asked to do is a direction you have not seen yet, and any strength you concentrate is strength you have gambled. That is one sentence, it is provable, and it fits in fifty lines. The teacher network, the stop-gradient and the hobbled predictor were never a method. They were scaffolding around a hole where a specification should have been, and once the specification exists the scaffolding is just something in the way.

Same blade at the end of it. No moon, no midnight, no counting to three hundred. Just a gauge, and something to hold it against.

And now you know. Fin.
