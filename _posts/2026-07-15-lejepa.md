*On the cover: a poured melt, cored along two dozen random directions at once. Decorative, and also roughly what SIGReg does a thousand times a step.*

In [part 1](/blog/jepa-nobody-cares-about-the-wallpaper/), we stopped asking vision models to repaint the scene and started asking them to describe it. We threw the wallpaper away, and the frozen-probe numbers said that was a good trade.

Then we looked at what was holding the whole thing up, and it was not pretty. There was a teacher network that is a slow moving average of the student. There was a stop-gradient, so the teacher never gets trained directly. And there was a predictor kept too weak to do the job alone. DINO adds a centering-and-sharpening schedule on top, which somebody tuned during a conference deadline and nobody has touched since.

None of those actually stops the model from answering "a person, probably" to every question. They just make it unlikely. And as we saw in part 1, the explanations for why it's unlikely came years after people started using them.

I like to think of the field as a blacksmith's workshop. The master makes really beautiful blades. He quenches them at a temperature his grandfather picked, and he counts three hundred hammer blows because three hundred is the number. But he couldn't tell you which of those steps actually matters. Ask him, and he'll start talking about the moon. Somebody once tried two hundred and ninety blows and the blade cracked in the fire, so now nobody tries anything.

Welcome to the era of **LeJEPA**. This is the story of how somebody finally wrote down the spec sheet.

## The charms are not free

You might think this is just a matter of taste. The blades come out fine and the papers get published, so who cares if the recipe is folklore?

Well, whoever pays for the GPUs cares.

The tricks all depend on each other. The teacher's decay rate interacts with the sharpening temperature. That interacts with the warmup length, which interacts with how narrow you made the predictor. So if you move one, you have to re-tune the others. You end up searching over all of them at once, and every point you try is a training run that costs GPU-months.

They also don't transfer. A recipe tuned for a ViT-L on ImageNet won't work for a ResNet on satellite images. And when it fails, it usually fails completely: the loss curve goes flat on day two and stays flat.

Worse, **you can't read the training loss.** In a teacher-student setup, the loss goes down whether the model is learning something or slowly collapsing toward the constant answer. So the only way to know if a run worked is to finish it, freeze the encoder, attach a probe and test it on labelled data. The whole selling point of self-supervised learning is that you don't need labels. And yet it couldn't tell you whether it was working without labels. It's like having a fuel gauge that's wired to the radio.

So to do this kind of research, you needed more than the GPUs for one run. You needed enough spare budget to burn a month of compute, twice, before you found out whether the recipe had worked.

## The anatomy of a LeJEPA

If you strip any joint-embedding method down, it is doing two jobs at once. It pulls together the descriptions of two views of the same image (a view is just a different crop or a colour-shifted copy of the picture). And it stops all the descriptions from piling up in the same spot.

The first family from part 1 does the second job with machinery: the EMA copy, the stop-gradient and the weakened predictor. [Barlow Twins](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.03230) and [VICReg](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.04906) do it with statistics instead. They put rules on how the descriptions are spread out across a batch. In part 1, I asked you to hold that thought.

Here's where it comes back. **LeJEPA** ([Balestriero and LeCun, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.08544)) belongs to that second family. But instead of hand-picked statistics, it uses the one target distribution you can prove is the right one. The name stands for Latent-Euclidean JEPA. One of its two authors is Yann LeCun, and I'm sure the "Le" is a coincidence.

The loss has two parts:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{LeJEPA}} = (1-\lambda)\, \mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}} + \lambda\, \mathcal{L}_{\text{SIGReg}}
$$

where $\lambda$ is a number between 0 and 1 that sets how much each part counts. It is the only knob in the loss. $\mathcal{L}_{\text{SIGReg}}$ is the new part, and most of this post is about it.

The prediction term is as plain as it gets. We take $V$ views of an image and shove each one through the same encoder, plus a small projector network on top, to get $z_1 \dots z_V$. Then we pull each one toward the average of all of them:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}} = \frac{1}{V}\sum_{v=1}^{V} \big\| z_v - \bar{z} \big\|^2, \qquad \bar{z} = \frac{1}{V}\sum_{u=1}^{V} z_u
$$

Look at $\bar{z}$ for a second. There's no stop-gradient on it, so the training signal flows back through the target too. Every method since [BYOL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.07733) said you must never do that.

![Two encoder branches sharing weights feed into a prediction term and a SIGReg term, with a line of crossed-out components below](/images/blog29/lejepa-architecture.png) *Figure 1: LeJEPA. One encoder, used on every view, with gradients flowing through all of them. Everything listed along the bottom used to be mandatory. The second loss term is what replaced the lot. Source: Author*

**NOTE:** SIGReg works on the projector's output, not the encoder's. The features you keep and use later come from the encoder, one step before the part SIGReg shapes. Every method in this family does this, and as far as I know, nobody has a clean explanation for why it helps.

## What a good description should look like

Let's say you are casting a metal part, and the drawing doesn't say what it gets bolted to. Maybe it goes on a hinge, or on a bracket that takes a load straight down. It might even go on something the customer hasn't designed yet and will phone you about on a Friday. All you know is how much metal you have.

What shape do you pour?

You definitely don't pour a long thin rod. The first load that comes in from the side snaps it, and nobody in the meeting afterwards will accept "the load came from the wrong direction" as an answer. You pour something that's equally strong whichever way it gets pulled, because you can't bet on a direction. That is, more or less, what the paper proves about embeddings.

Here's the formal version. First, fix the total amount of spread in the embeddings (the trace of their covariance matrix). Otherwise the model could cheat by making every number bigger. That's your fixed amount of metal. Now think about all the downstream tasks you might be asked to do with a simple probe on top: a linear layer, a kernel method or $k$-nearest neighbours. The paper proves that one distribution keeps those probes' bias lowest across tasks, and that's the **isotropic Gaussian**, $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)$.

"Isotropic" means it has the same spread in every direction, which takes care of the direction gamble. That alone is enough for a linear probe. For the kernel and $k$-NN probes, the shape also has to be the familiar bell curve, the Gaussian. Out of all distributions with a given spread, the Gaussian is the one with the [most entropy](/blog/cross-entropy-loss/). That's a fancy way of saying it assumes nothing you didn't ask for.

Brace yourselves, here's some arithmetic to make it concrete.

Take an embedding with 512 numbers in it (512 dimensions), and a total spread budget of 512 units. Cast A puts 64 units into each of 8 directions and nothing into the other 504. Cast B puts 1 unit into every direction. Now a downstream task shows up, and it needs to read some direction $w$ that you didn't know about during training. Say $w$ is one of the 512 axes, picked at random. Under Cast A, the chance that it's one of the 8 with any signal is 8 in 512, about 1.6%. The other 98.4% of the time, your probe is looking at a constant and learns nothing. Under Cast B, every direction the task could ask for has a spread of 1.

![Two point clouds with the same total variance, one squashed flat and one round, each showing its spread along a vertical probe direction](/images/blog29/isotropy-argument.png) *Figure 2: The same amount of metal, poured two ways. Both clouds have the same total variance. The flat one has 0.15 of spread along the direction the downstream task happened to want; the round one has 0.99, and would have 0.99 along any other direction you picked. Source: Author*

Real embeddings are lopsided by degrees, not all-or-nothing like Cast A, so real numbers are less dramatic than 1.6%. But Cast B came out ahead in every version of this calculation I tried.

## SIGReg, or: you cannot X-ray an ingot

Fine, so we aim for the isotropic Gaussian. Now how do we check whether we got there, you ask?

This is where it should fall apart. Testing whether a cloud of points in 512 dimensions follows a particular distribution is famously hard. The batch you're testing might only have 512 points in it. And you need to run the test on every step of training, for a hundred epochs.

The way out was published in 1936 by two statisticians with, I am confident, no GPUs between them. The **Cramér–Wold** theorem says you can pin down a distribution in many dimensions just by looking at its one-dimensional shadows. Pick a direction $w$, project every point onto it ($w^\top z$), and look at the histogram you get. If you know that histogram for every possible direction, you know the whole distribution. For us, that means $z$ is an isotropic Gaussian exactly when *every* one of its 1D shadows is the same bell curve, $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$, whichever way you point.

You can't X-ray the ingot, but you can drill core samples out of it. If every core comes out right, so does the ingot.

### Step 1: the core sample

We draw $K$ random directions and scale each one to length 1. We stack them into a matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times K}$. Then we project the whole batch onto all of them at once, $P = ZA$, which is a single matrix multiply. $K$ is 1024 in the paper's ImageNet runs and 256 in their minimal example.

We obviously can't check every direction, because there are infinitely many. So we draw a fresh set of $K$ on every training step. The random seed is the step number, so every GPU picks the same directions. Over a whole run, you end up covering all the directions. And the model never gets to learn which directions the inspector likes to check (models, like students, study for the exam they expect).

### Step 2: tap it and listen

Now we need a test for one direction at a time. We use the characteristic function of a distribution, $\varphi(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{itX}]$, which is its Fourier transform. You can think of it as the sound a sample makes when you tap it. Two distributions are the same exactly when they ring the same at every frequency $t$. For the standard bell curve, the ring is $\varphi(t) = e^{-t^2/2}$. We know that in advance, so there's nothing to estimate.

The **Epps–Pulley** statistic measures the gap between the sample's ring and that one. In words first:

$$
T = N \times \sum_{\text{frequencies}} \big(\text{how far off the sample rings there}\big)^2 \times \big(\text{how much that frequency counts}\big)
$$

specifically,

$$
T = N \int \big| \hat{\varphi}(t) - e^{-t^2/2} \big|^2 \, e^{-t^2/2} \, dt
$$

where $N$ is the number of samples, $\hat{\varphi}(t) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} e^{i t p_n}$ is the empirical characteristic function of the projected values $p_n$, and the second $e^{-t^2/2}$ is the weight, which says low frequencies count for more than high ones.

In the code, that integral becomes a sum over 17 evenly spaced points between $t=-5$ and $t=5$. Seventeen. Not sixteen, not twenty. Somebody ran the ablation over 5, 17 and 41 and came back with 17, and I have more faith in that number than in any round one.

![The target curve e to the minus t squared over two against a sample's empirical characteristic function, and the resulting integrand](/images/blog29/epps-pulley.png) *Figure 3: Left, the standard normal's frequency signature in black and a projected sample's in red, read at 17 points. Right, the weighted squared gap between them. Its area, times the batch size, is what gets added to the loss. The gap is symmetric around $t=0$, so only the positive half is drawn; the code spreads its 17 points from $-5$ to $5$. Source: Author*

The library also ships Anderson–Darling, Cramér–von Mises, Shapiro–Wilk, Jarque–Bera and Watson, so picking Epps–Pulley was a choice. Why that one? It's built from $\cos$ and $\sin$ of the data, and those never go above 1. So the loss and its gradient stay bounded too. Kolmogorov–Smirnov makes no such promise. And a penalty that can blow up on one bad minibatch is a loss spike waiting to happen. A statistician runs a normality test once, on a finished dataset. Here we're training through one four hundred thousand times.

![A point cloud with seven random directions marked, and two of the projected histograms compared against a normal curve](/images/blog29/sigreg-slicing.png) *Figure 4: Slicing in action. Direction 1 gives a projection that passes for Gaussian and contributes almost nothing to the loss. Direction 2 catches the same cloud being two lumps and is where the gradient comes from. Source: Author*

### Step 3: the dial

Average the statistic over the $K$ directions, multiply by $\lambda$, add the prediction term times $(1 - \lambda)$, and you have the whole loss. $\lambda$ is 0.05 in the ImageNet runs and 0.02 in the small one. That's about fifty lines of code with one number to pick. I have watched grown engineers lose a fortnight to six of them.

There's one detail here I really like. Training runs on hundreds of GPUs, and each one holds a slice of the batch. To compute the statistic over the whole batch, the GPUs only need to share their averages of $\cos(t p_n)$ and $\sin(t p_n)$ at each of the 17 points. That's 34 numbers per direction, whether the global batch is 512 images or 512,000. So the test is exact over every sample on every GPU, and it costs less network traffic than your laptop spends telling a server it is still awake.

## What that buys

The paper reports the recipe working out of the box on more than 60 architectures (ResNets, ViTs, ConvNets and more), more than 10 datasets, and models up to 1.8B parameters. Nothing was tuned per architecture.

Here's how it does against part 1's I-JEPA. The scores are averaged over eight other datasets, with one labelled example per class, ten per class, and all the labels:

| Model | Params | Epochs | 1-shot | 10-shot | all labels |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| LeJEPA ViT-L | 304M | 100 | 29.55 | **60.95** | **79.48** |
| LeJEPA ConvNeXtV2-H | 660M | 100 | **31.58** | 60.70 | 77.56 |
| I-JEPA ViT-H | 632M | 300 | 30.20 | 60.51 | 78.50 |

Nobody wins that table. The margins are all under a point, and I wouldn't defend any of them. But that's the point. LeJEPA's ViT-L has half the parameters of I-JEPA's ViT-H and trained for a third of the epochs. It drops the teacher, the stop-gradient, the predictor and the schedules from part 1. And it still ties.

Scale it up and a ViT-H/14 gets 79% on ImageNet-1K with a frozen linear probe. DINOv2's ViT-g/14 gets 86.5%. Seven and a half points is not a rounding error, and I won't pretend it is.

The number I care about most is a different one. Across runs with different settings, LeJEPA's training loss ranks the models almost the same way their final probe accuracy does. The rank correlation (Spearman) between the two is about 85%. If you divide the loss by a power of $\lambda$ ($\lambda^{0.4}$), it goes up to about 99%. So you can compare runs just by looking at the loss, before training has finished and without a single label. You don't have to wait three days to find out the run was dead on Tuesday. After five years of a number on the screen that told you nothing, somebody finally wired the fuel gauge to the tank.

That makes this kind of research a lot cheaper to get into. Their worked example gets a ViT-S/8 to 90.7% on [Imagenette](https://github.com/fastai/imagenette) (a 10-class slice of ImageNet) in about 200 minutes on one GPU. And pretraining on your own small dataset starts to make sense. On Galaxy10, which has roughly 11,000 pictures of galaxies, pretraining from scratch with LeJEPA beats starting from DINOv2. Eleven thousand images beat a model that ate the internet, and I read that table twice to be sure.

| Piece | I-JEPA and friends | LeJEPA | What it was for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Target encoder | EMA copy, $\tau \approx 0.996$–$0.999$ | none | stopping the branches agreeing on nothing |
| Gradient path | stop-grad on the target | gradients through all views | same |
| Predictor | narrow ViT, weak on purpose | none | same |
| Output schedule | centering and sharpening | none | same |
| What forbids collapse | nothing, empirically | the SIGReg term | — |
| Knobs in the loss | several, coupled | one, $\lambda$ | — |

You can even run the ablation in your head. Set $\lambda = 0$ and only the prediction term is left. Its best possible score comes from the constant embedding, which is part 1's representation collapse turning up right on cue. These models are lazy animals. Leave a free lunch in the loss, and gradient descent will have found it before you've finished typing `wandb.init`. The difference now is that you can see it coming from the algebra, instead of from a dead run on Thursday morning.

## Where things are going

**LeWorldModel (2026)**: [LeWorldModel](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19312) puts SIGReg into an action-conditioned world model (part 1's tier 3), trained end to end from pixels. Its loss has only two terms: predict the next embedding, and keep the embeddings Gaussian. The previous end-to-end approach had six loss settings to tune, and this one has one. It has about 15M parameters, trains on a single GPU, and plans up to 48x faster than world models built on big pretrained backbones. That's part 1's tier 3 without the machinery. It's also the cheap end of the [world-models-as-data-substrate](/blog/vla-pixels-to-tokens/) bet the VLA post closed on. Then [When Does LeJEPA Learn a World Model?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26379) takes on the question part 1 spent a whole section being pedantic about.

**Arguing with the target**: people are now pushing back on the isotropic Gaussian, and I think that's a good sign. You can only argue with a target once somebody has written one down. [Beyond Isotropy in JEPAs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.20107) proposes a Hamiltonian geometry instead. SPHERE-JEPA puts the embeddings on a sphere. KerJEPA swaps the characteristic-function test for a kernel-based one. I'd much rather have people arguing about the target than the old situation, where there was no target and everyone just added another trick.

**Video**: LeVJEPA brings the recipe to video. That's where the savings should matter most, because video is where tuning all those tricks always cost the most.

## What is still folklore

SIGReg controls the *shape of the cloud*. It says nothing about what should count as two views of the same image. That's still decided the way it was in 2021: 2 big crops, 6 small crops, colour jitter, grayscale, blur and solarisation. Every threshold and probability in there was copied from DINO, and none of them has been re-derived. Each of those numbers is somebody's Tuesday afternoon. If I could have a proof for only one half of the recipe, I'd honestly have picked this half.

Being seven and a half points behind DINOv2 also tells you who this is for. If you want the best frozen ImageNet features on earth, go and download DINOv2. It's right there and it's free. LeJEPA is for when nobody has pretrained a model on data like yours, and nobody is going to. Maybe your data is 11,000 galaxies, or a radiology archive you can't upload anywhere.

The sliced test is also weaker in high dimensions than the Cramér–Wold theorem makes it sound. As the number of dimensions $D$ grows, random 1D shadows of almost any distribution start to look Gaussian. Diaconis and Freedman made this precise in 1984. So a cloud can pass a great many 1D normality tests while being badly non-Gaussian in ways no single direction shows.

![Histograms of a random one-dimensional projection of a uniform hypercube at three dimensions, becoming more bell-shaped as the dimension rises](/images/blog29/projection-power.png) *Figure 5: Points drawn uniformly from a hypercube, which is about as un-Gaussian as a distribution gets, projected onto one random direction. At $D=2$ the test would catch it instantly. At $D=128$ the projection is visually indistinguishable from the bell curve it is being compared against. Source: Author*

The theorem is still true, because it covers *all* directions and you're drawing 1,024 fresh ones every step. The paper's answer is that a smooth network doesn't produce adversarial embeddings, so a modest number of directions still catches real problems. I believe that. I'd still like to see somebody map out when it fails, instead of arguing about it.

And the "provable" part is about the target, not about getting there. The theorem says an isotropic Gaussian embedding is the best one for downstream tasks. It doesn't say that gradient descent on this penalty will actually reach one, and there's no convergence guarantee. What changed is that the loss now says where it's trying to go. That's still a lot better than a loss whose best possible score goes to a model that learned nothing.

## Conclusion

Part 1 argued that reconstruction was the wrong thing to ask for. You describe the room instead of repainting it, because nobody cares about the wallpaper. But part 1 couldn't say what a good description was. It could only point at a bad one.

This post was the other half. A good description is spread out evenly in every direction. You don't know yet which direction the downstream task will need, so piling everything into a few directions is a bet you don't have to make. You can prove that. And using it costs one extra term in the loss and about fifty lines of Python. The teacher network, the stop-gradient and the weakened predictor were all workarounds for not knowing this.

The smith still makes good blades. But the next time an apprentice asks him why three hundred blows, somebody in the room can actually answer.

And now you know. Fin.
